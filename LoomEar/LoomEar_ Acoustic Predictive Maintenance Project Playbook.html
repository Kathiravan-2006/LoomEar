  1. Open your project folder as the workspace, with the project rules file in place (below).
  2. Paste the phase prompt. Use Planning mode for any phase that creates or restructures many files, and Fast mode for small fixes.
  3. Read the implementation plan the agent produces before you approve it. Change anything that does not match your scope.
  4. Let it run, then read the task list and walkthrough it leaves behind. Open the figures and logs yourself.
  5. Run the tests. Commit to Git with a message naming the phase.

Project foundation

Title and tagline

LoomEar: Acoustic Predictive Maintenance for Power Looms using Cycle-Synchronous Audio Analytics and Anomaly Detection.

A microphone, a model and a maintenance decision, delivered before the breakdown.

Problem statement

Power looms run for long shifts at hundreds of picks per minute. Bearings wear, belts slip, timing drifts and lubrication fails, and each of these changes the machine's sound well before it stops. Most mills still rely on breakdown repair or fixed-interval servicing, which causes unplanned downtime, fabric defects and wasted maintenance. Vibration sensors work but are costly to install on every loom. A low-cost microphone can capture the same early warnings, but factory noise, many neighbouring machines and scarce fault labels make the problem hard.

Objectives

  1. Build a reproducible pipeline that records, cleans and segments loom audio into pick cycles.
  2. Engineer time, frequency and cycle-synchronous features that capture early mechanical degradation.
  3. Train anomaly detectors that learn from healthy sound alone, and classifiers for known fault types where labels exist.
  4. Convert model output into a stable 0 to 100 health index with Healthy, Watch and Act states.
  5. Prove generalisation by testing on machines the model has never heard.
  6. Deliver a dashboard, an API and an edge-device prototype, and quantify the maintenance savings.

Scope

In scope

  • Airborne sound from one or more looms (rapier, projectile, air-jet or shuttle, state which)
  • Anomaly detection, fault classification, health index
  • Offline evaluation and a near-real-time demo

Out of scope

  • Fabric defect detection by camera
  • Remaining useful life prediction as a guaranteed number (shown as a trend only)
  • Production-grade deployment across a whole mill

The unique idea: pick-cycle-synchronous analysis

A loom is periodic. Every pick (one pass of weft) repeats the same sequence of mechanical events: shedding, weft insertion, beat-up. Most audio projects chop sound into arbitrary fixed windows and lose this structure. LoomEar does three things differently:

  1. Find the pick period. Estimate it from the autocorrelation of the signal envelope.
  2. Fold the audio into cycles. Each cycle is time-warped to a fixed length so cycle position means the same event on every pick.
  3. Compare against a healthy template. Average many healthy cycles into a template. The residual between a live cycle and the template shows where in the pick and at what frequency something has changed.

That residual doubles as an explanation: a heatmap you can show a mechanic that says "extra energy at beat-up, 3 to 6 kHz, growing for two weeks". Few student projects can offer that.

Data plan

The hardest part of this project is data. Fault recordings are rare and mills do not hand them out. So you use three sources, each for a different job, and you label them honestly.

SourceRoleDetails
Real loom recordingsPrimary evidenceHealthy baseline over several days and shifts, plus any faulty or pre-maintenance periods. Pair every session with the maintenance log.
Public machine-sound benchmarksValidate your method where fault labels existDatasets such as MIMII (pumps, fans, valves, slide rails) and the DCASE anomalous sound detection challenge data. Check each licence and cite correctly.
Synthetic loom simulatorControlled fault injection and augmentationPeriodic impact trains at the pick rate, with injected fault signatures and mixed with recorded mill ambience at chosen signal-to-noise ratios. Always labelled synthetic.

Recording protocol

  • Microphone 30 to 50 cm from the beat-up and main drive zone, on a fixed tripod, same position every session. Record the position in metadata.
  • Mono or stereo WAV, 16-bit, 44.1 or 48 kHz. Keep a raw copy untouched.
  • Sessions of 10 minutes or more, repeated across shifts and days, to capture normal variation in fabric, speed and ambient noise.
  • Log for each session: machine ID, loom type, rpm or picks per minute, fabric and yarn, shift, microphone position, maintenance status, and who labelled it.
  • Record a mill-ambience clip with the loom off, for noise augmentation.
  • Safety and ethics: stay clear of moving parts, wear hearing protection, get written permission from the mill, and remove or avoid recording identifiable speech.
If you cannot get fault data. That is normal. Frame the study as healthy-only anomaly detection on real data, validate on public benchmarks, and use simulation for fault stress tests. Say this plainly in the limitations section. Honesty scores better than a fake fault set.

Metadata schema

Every audio file gets one row: file_id, source_type (real/public/synthetic), machine_id, loom_type, rpm, session_id, date, shift, mic_position, condition (healthy/watch/fault), fault_type, label_source (log/expert/injected), snr_db, notes.

Method

Pipeline stages

  1. Acquire. Load audio, validate sample rate and duration, drop corrupt files.
  2. Clean. Resample to 16 or 22.05 kHz, apply a band-pass (for example 50 Hz to 8 kHz), remove DC offset, and normalise per recording. Optionally reduce stationary noise, and compare with and without to prove it helps.
  3. Segment into pick cycles. Envelope, autocorrelation, period estimate, then fold and time-normalise.
  4. Extract features. Handcrafted, log-mel spectrogram, and cycle-synchronous residual.
  5. Model. Baselines, then deep models, then optional pretrained embeddings.
  6. Score and decide. Anomaly score to health index, smoothing, thresholds, alerts.
  7. Explain and evaluate. Metrics, robustness, heatmaps, SHAP.
  8. Serve. API, dashboard, edge prototype, cost simulation.

Health index and decision logic

  1. Compute the anomaly score for each cycle.
  2. Convert to a 0 to 100 index using percentiles of healthy validation scores, so 50 means "typical of the healthy upper range" rather than an arbitrary number.
  3. Smooth with an exponentially weighted moving average, and require several consecutive high readings before changing state (hysteresis), to avoid false alarms.
  4. States: Healthy, Watch, Act. Choose thresholds on validation data to hit a target false-alarm rate, and state that rate in the report.
  5. Plot the index over days. A rising trend is the predictive part of predictive maintenance.

Evaluation design

QuestionTestMetric

Business case

Estimate savings with a simple, transparent model. Let Cd be the cost per hour of unplanned downtime, Hu the unplanned downtime hours per loom per year, d the fraction of those failures caught early by the system, and Cs the cost of the sensing system per loom per year. Annual saving per loom is roughly d × Hu × Cd − Cs − false-alarm cost. Use real figures from your mill contact, state your assumptions, and show a sensitivity table.

Preprocessing and pick-cycle segmentation

Output: cleaned audio, cycle extractor, healthy templates. This is your signature contribution.

Prompt 4: cycle-synchronous analysis
Implement the pick-cycle-synchronous pipeline in src/loomear/audio/.

preprocess.py
- Resample, band-pass, DC removal, per-file normalisation, optional stationary noise reduction controlled by config.

cycles.py
1. Compute the signal envelope (Hilbert or rectified and low-passed).
2. Estimate the pick period using autocorrelation of the envelope, constrained to a plausible range from the logged rpm. Return a confidence value, and flag low-confidence files.
3. Detect cycle boundaries by anchoring on the strongest recurring impact (the beat-up event), tolerating drift.
4. Extract each cycle and time-normalise it to a fixed number of samples (configurable) so position inside the cycle is comparable.
5. Build a healthy template per machine: the median or mean log-mel spectrogram over many healthy cycles, plus a per-bin standard deviation.
6. Compute the residual map for any cycle: (cycle minus template) divided by standard deviation.

Validation and figures
- Plot detected boundaries over the waveform for several files.
- Plot the template and an example residual for healthy versus each synthetic fault.
- Report how accurately the estimated period matches the known period in synthetic data.
- Compare against naive fixed-length windows: does cycle alignment reduce healthy-data variance? Quantify it.

Add unit tests with synthetic signals of known period, including jitter and added noise. Write docs/phase_04_summary.md explaining the method in plain English with a diagram description I can redraw.

Rescue prompts

Keep these ready for when something goes wrong.

Results look too good
My model reports unusually high performance. Audit for data leakage. Check: frames from one recording in both train and test, scalers or thresholds fitted before splitting, machine or session identity being learnable from recording conditions (microphone gain, background hum, shift), duplicated files, and labels derived from features. Write a short test for each and report which pass or fail. Do not change any results until I have reviewed your findings.
Results look too bad
Performance is poor. Diagnose step by step before changing the model: check audio loading and sample rate, check pick-period estimation confidence, compare healthy versus faulty feature distributions for separability, check class balance and label quality, check whether the noise level swamps the signal. Rank the likely causes and test the top three with the smallest possible experiments. Show me the evidence for each.
Explain to me
Explain the code you just wrote as if I must defend it in a viva tomorrow. For each major function, give: what it does, why this approach was chosen over the obvious alternative, what could go wrong, and one question an examiner might ask. Keep it plain and specific to this project.

Abstract template
Unplanned breakdowns of power looms cause costly downtime, fabric defects and inefficient maintenance, yet continuous vibration monitoring of every machine is often too expensive for small and medium mills. This project presents LoomEar, a low-cost acoustic predictive maintenance system that listens to a loom through a single microphone and estimates its mechanical health. Audio was gathered from [number] looms of type [type] over [duration], supplemented by the public [benchmark name] dataset and a physically motivated synthetic loom simulator used for controlled fault injection. A pick-cycle-synchronous method estimates the loom's pick period, folds the signal into time-normalised cycles, and compares each cycle with a healthy template to produce a residual map that shows when in the pick and at which frequencies the sound has changed. Handcrafted features, log-mel spectrograms and residuals were used to train classical anomaly detectors, a convolutional autoencoder and fault classifiers. On held-out sessions the best model achieved [metric] at a false-alarm rate of [value], and on leave-one-machine-out testing it achieved [metric], showing [conclusion about generalisation]. Anomaly scores were converted into a 0 to 100 health index with Healthy, Watch and Act states, giving a mean warning lead time of [value] before logged failures. The system was deployed as a FastAPI service, a Streamlit dashboard and a Raspberry Pi prototype with [latency] scoring time. A cost model suggests annual savings of [range] per loom under stated assumptions. The work shows that [one-sentence takeaway], and identifies limitations in [data size, number of machines, label quality] for future work.

Viva preparation

Open each question and practise answering aloud before you read the model answer. Then adapt the answers to your real results.

Why sound instead of vibration?

Microphones are cheap, easy to fit without touching the machine, and they pick up many fault types in one sensor. Vibration sensors are more direct but cost more and need mounting on each machine. My work shows how far a microphone alone can go, and I list the trade-off honestly.

How do you handle noise from neighbouring looms?

Close microphone placement, a band-pass filter, noise-mixing augmentation at several signal-to-noise ratios, and a robustness curve showing performance as noise rises. Per-machine templates also absorb each position's constant background.

What is new in your work?

Pick-cycle-synchronous analysis. Instead of arbitrary windows, I align audio to the loom's own cycle and compare against a healthy template, which both improves detection and explains where in the pick the change happened.

Why anomaly detection rather than classification?

Fault examples are rare and new faults appear unannounced. Learning what healthy sounds like needs no fault labels and can flag unseen problems. Classification is added where labels exist, to tell the mechanic what kind of fault it is.

How do you know you have no data leakage?

I split by session and by machine, fit all scalers and thresholds on training data only, and run an automated audit. I also test on leave-one-machine-out, which a leaking pipeline cannot pass.

Is the synthetic data valid evidence?

It is used for controlled stress tests and augmentation, never mixed into real-data metrics, and always labelled. Real and public results are reported separately.

How is the health index defined and why those thresholds?

It is a percentile mapping of the anomaly score against healthy validation data. Thresholds were chosen on validation data to reach a target false-alarm rate, with smoothing and hysteresis to avoid flapping alerts.

Would this work on a different loom type?

The method transfers, but the template and threshold must be learned per machine. My leave-one-machine-out result shows how much performance drops without that calibration, and I recommend a short calibration recording on each new loom.

What are the limitations?

Limited number of machines and fault examples, label quality from maintenance logs, and a single microphone position. Future work includes more mills, multi-microphone arrays and fusion with current or vibration sensing.

How would a mill actually use this?

A low-cost edge unit per loom scores audio continuously and sends a health index to a dashboard. Supervisors see Healthy, Watch and Act states and a ranked fleet list, and maintenance is scheduled for Watch machines before they fail.
